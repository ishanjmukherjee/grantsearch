Demystifying Northwestern University Libraries: A Guide on Utilizing Resources to Their Full
Capacity
Whether you’re pursuing a personal research project or simply writing a paper for a class, seeking out the
right resources to fit your needs can be a challenge. One obvious yet underrated source of information is
to go to one of the many libraries that call Northwestern University (N.U.) “home”. Libraries are often
seen as a social and study space by many students, and the vast array of resources that they offer can be
overlooked or simply unknown. When was the last time you picked up one of the books in a Deering
Library reading room or explored past the second floor of Main Library? I spoke to Engagement Librarian
Jason Krause and Special Collections Librarian at the McCormick Library of Special Collections and
University Archives Jason Nargis to find out how students can make the most of everything the libraries
have to offer.
Special Collections can seem intimidating, especially since the items available there are one-of-a-kind.
There is a common misconception that these collections are only for a specialized audience, but Nargis
emphasizes that the environment is very user-friendly and focused on giving students a hands-on
experience of interacting with the materials. Everything from vintage comic books, to an archive of 
 N.U.’s publications and football tapes, to artifacts from the 20th-century avant-garde movement can be
found here for research purposes or for general enjoyment. The materials in Special Collections are not
circulated and are difficult to digitize, so accessing them requires deliberate efforts to go visit the space.
Though this could be seen as a disadvantage to students used to finding all they need at the click of a
 button, seeing the materials and engaging with them in person adds immense value to the experience — 
 Nargis explained that these artifacts contain information, not only in terms of its context and content, but
also within the object itself, that is only possible to convey in person. Nargis says that engaging with
historical objects, getting a “sense of the texture of the leather or the paper” can make “history feel alive
… and that there were actual people involved.” These rare materials are especially incredible to have
access to, as they are the primary sources used by the authors of books we might study in a history class
 — this is “where the argument begins.” Nargis says that by engaging with these resources, students can
“develop their own conclusions from it, and their interpretations and hypotheses are just as valid as
anyone else’s.”
Aside from the more traditional forms of resources offered by the libraries, there are also support systems
constantly available for students at any stage in their research. An example of this are the subject
librarians — experts that can help students with anything from finding a research question to citing
sources through research consultations, which can be booked through the library website or by contacting
the librarian directly. Getting direct help from someone who is not only an expert in your field but also
familiar with all the resources to which you have access can be a valuable asset to your research journey.
Furthermore, there are data support services available through the N.U. Geographic Information System
(GIS) services, which provide consultations and workshops to guide students through data analysis
software in addition to the workshops and events organized by the library to provide support in various
areas, including data analysis.
On top of this, there are a multitude of spaces in the libraries that are equipped with tools to fit your 
needs. The study rooms and meeting rooms in Main Library and Mudd Library are perfect for

collaborating with others, and the vocal booth in Main Library and the video production studio in Mudd
Library can be used for professional-quality audio/visual projects. The computers in all of the libraries
contain software that students may otherwise need to purchase, such as Adobe Suite and data analysis
software. The libraries also loan out a host of equipment, from the humble phone charger and umbrella to
iPads, laptops, and Apple Pencils, as well as a variety of camera equipment.
The library is truly bursting with a plethora of resources that are just waiting to be put to use by students;
 beyond the ones listed here, there are so many more to explore. Both librarians agree that the best advice
for any student starting out and exploring their options is to just ask a librarian for help, even if it doesn’t
seem like a library-related question. Kruse stresses that the library is highly interwoven with so many
other offices and departments in N.U. that “if we [the librarians] don’t have the answer, we’ll get you to
who you need to talk to.”
Kruse also wants to encourage students to approach librarians and ask questions because of the
characteristic imposter syndrome in colleges like N.U. that can hinder students’ abilities to ask for help:
“There’s this hesitancy in colleges, in particular in places like N.U., [to ask questions, because] there’s
this feeling that ‘everyone around me already knows the answers to these questions [and] how to do all
these things,’ so nobody’s asking.” But he believes that by finding people to guide you in your research
 process, it can take the extra weight of navigating the “non-intuitive and sometimes convoluted
 processes” that can come with research off of students, allowing them to focus solely on the content of 
their studies.
