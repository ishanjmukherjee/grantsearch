The road to research: an interview with Dr. Andrew Miri, assistant professor in the
neurobiology department and neural dynamics researcher
The goal of this interview is to provide insight into a career in research as well as what life
experiences prompted Dr. Miri to this profession.
Background:
Q:
 Can you tell me a little about yourself?
A:
 “I’m
 Andrew Miri
. I'm an assistant professor in the neurobiology department at
 Northwestern. I've been here for about five and a half years. My lab studies motor control, which
is a common theme through my work since graduate school. But things have changed a bit over 
time…”
Dr. Miri shared
 he didn’t always know he wanted to pursue a career in research
.
When entering Brown for his undergraduate education, he admitted he thought he would become
a doctor.
However, after working in a lab the summer after his freshman year, he realized research felt like
a “better fit for [him] for a lot of reasons than becoming a doctor”.
Yet, after his college graduation, he ultimately chose to temporarily pursue teaching: “My first
 job [post college] was actually as a high school and middle school math teacher. I taught 8th and
10th grade math at a public charter school in Boston. I was very interested in education and
thought about going to grad school”.
Education or science?
“I didn’t know whether I wanted to go to graduate school for education policy or science. I was
kind of like, you know, flipping a coin. Anyway, I ended up going for science. Dr. Miri still does
“a little bit of teaching”... He teaches Neuroscience 206: Systems and Behavioral Neuroscience
as well as a graduate school course. When asked about the balance between research and
teaching in his life, he stated about fifteen percent of his time and effort is devoted to teaching
although he admits “it’s a complicated estimation”.
However, he maintains that the two, teaching and research, “absolutely complement each
other”... With teaching, “professors must figure out what they want to convey to students… At
this level, it’s not immediately apparent what students should know, so there’s some construction
of knowledge. This is very much related to the work we do as researchers, trying to construct
new ideas”.

When asked to provide more information about why he ultimately chose science over education,
he shared the following: “I chose science because I like more what you do on a day-to-day
 basis…as opposed to education policy, where the ideas and issues seemed more urgent and
interesting, but what do you do on a day to day basis?”
He also attributes much of his decision to ultimately pursue research to his undergraduate advisor 
Anita: “
Most people, including myself, show up to college and they really have no idea what
it means to be a scientist.
 Maybe some people have scientist parents….I certainly didn’t.
 The
real issue is that if you haven’t really known a scientist and you don’t really know what it
means to do science, it’s hard to envision yourself doing that for a career…
It’s not until you
have an experience doing research with an advisor that you see what research your advisor is
doing, you do it with them, and you come to understand [research] is something you could
actually pursue”
He ended our discussion with the following remarks: “One of the issues for college students in
their young 20s is that you must make a first guess about a career path…maybe after an
internship or maybe after taking a few courses… but certainly after limited experience. You
choose a career path without really knowing what it’s going to be like…
I think science is somewhat unique in the way that it changes as you keep doing it on a long time
scale. Students start out in research and they basically do something where somebody else has
done the thinking for them. That’s a good way to get a feel for science, but that's really not what
science is. As you build expertise, you start to do more of the thinking behind the work, and so it
 becomes more creative and rewarding.”
