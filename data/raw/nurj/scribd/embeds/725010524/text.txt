Creating a Musical Narrative: A Look Into Eighteenth-Century Music and Society with Dr.
Andrew Talle
Dr. Andrew Talle is an Associate Professor of Musicology at the Bienen School of Music of 
Northwestern University (N.U.). In 1995, he earned a B.M. in cello performance and a B.A./M.A.
in linguistics from N.U., and went on to acquire a Ph.D. in musicology from Harvard University.
Dr. Talle’s research interests cover musical culture at the time of Johann Sebastian Bach. Fluent 
in German, he has published articles in German musicological journals as well as his 2017 book 
Beyond Bach: Music and Everyday Life in the Eighteenth Century.
 Dr. Talle’s current projects
include a monograph about Bach’s music for solo violin and solo cello and a collection of 
accounts of the city of Leipzig written by eighteenth-century travelers.
1. Could you tell us a little about yourself and your current research interests?
I grew up in Minnesota and started playing the cello when I was three. I came to N.U. for college
as a dual degree student in cello performance and linguistics. After completing a Ph.D. in
musicology at Harvard University, I taught at the Peabody Conservatory of Johns Hopkins
University for 13 years. In 2017, after more than 20 years away, I arrived back at N.U. to teach
musicology. My research centers around Johann Sebastian Bach and the musical culture of his
time and place.
2. When did you begin undertaking serious musicology research?
In musicology Ph.D. programs, you take classes on a variety of musical eras and topics with
various faculty members, each of whom has a somewhat different approach. I took a class with
renowned Bach scholar Christoph Wolff. I liked his teaching style and so decided to work with
him. I grew up playing Bach’s cello suites and wanted to learn more about this music, which is
shrouded in mystery. My dissertation research took me to Leipzig, Germany, where I initially
spent three years from 2000 to 2003. There I had access to a vast array of literature about
Bach, but also got to meet the people who were doing this research at the highest level. While
classes offered some context, my in-person experience in Germany enlightened me the most.
3. Do you have a specific “algorithm” when tackling research questions?
No – I just follow the evidence where it leads. One always has a theory in mind, but one has to
be open to that theory being contradicted or changed. In my experience, I very rarely found
evidence supporting the theory I initially went out to test – I will almost always find contradictory
evidence or something that tweaks my original theory. Sometimes, I find something totally
unrelated to my theory that leads my research in a totally different and more interesting
direction. I know that a project won’t be complete until I have figured out, to my satisfaction,
where the evidence actually leads.
4. In your book “Beyond Bach,” you pieced together an anthology of stories about people
surrounding J. S. Bach. It was very interesting to see the pieces fall together to create a

narrative. What is the source-collecting and analysis process like? Could you walk me
through it?
My goal for that book was to reconstruct the lives of music lovers in Bach’s time without putting
Bach in the spotlight. He is too often the reference point when tracing the lives and experiences
of other musical figures of the time. The music of amateur musicians or children is something to
which researchers have not paid enough attention.
Musicologists have historically focused on the “supply side” of music production, striving to
uncover something about the composer’s creative process and learn how the music we love
came to be. I wanted to focus on the “demand side”: the music that people really wanted to hear 
and how Bach’s music related to that. I did not want to presume that Bach was at the center of 
their musical world the way he is for classical musicians today. The people of Bach’s era did not
see themselves as Bach’s contemporaries; they were stars in their own private dramas, and I
wanted to explore some of those dramas in my book.
I chose the characters in
 Beyond Bach
 because their lives were the best documented. These
people are normally seen as “extras” in music history, and I wanted to make them stars. I chose
the people whose extant information was the richest, from diaries and music manuscripts to
paintings, letters, instruments, and account books. I was able to tell stories about them that I
could not tell about Bach, simply because we do not have nearly enough information about
Bach. This allowed me to really delve into the details of how these people lived. Chapter 11 of 
Beyond Bach
 is based on an account book of an organist, a contemporary of Bach, who kept
track of every penny he earned and spent over 13 years. It is the kind of documentation that we
have for nobody else. The available details make these people come to life.
If you want to do research on a musician of a past era, it certainly helps to go to the place where
they were once active, learn the language, and see the documents available only there. While a
lot of documents relating to European classical music ended up in the United States, they tend
to come as little isolated “flowers” that have been plucked from the ground in which they grew.
You need the climate, the soil, and the weeds of the original location to do comprehensive,
original research.
5. It seems like much of the work put into
 Beyond Bach
 was interdisciplinary, with a variety
of specialized knowledge from sociology, literature, and philosophy blended within your 
interpretation of this musical narrative of the eighteenth century. Did you collaborate with
other such experts, and what was that experience like?
I have collaborated quite a bit with experts on the history of Leipzig, both in Germany and
elsewhere. Some were very important mentors to me when I was a student. That said, there are
certain skills that you have to master in order to do such research. First, you have to learn how
to read, speak, and understand German, and to read old German handwriting. You also need to
be comfortable dealing with German scholars and archivists. Obtaining valuable information
becomes much easier if you interact with these people in person.

One thing that nobody told me while I was in graduate school was the importance of diplomacy
in research. You have to be comfortable dealing with librarians and archivists in a way that is
rewarding for them too. It is a two-way street; they also have to feel appreciated and valuable
for your project.
6. Do you have any advice for students who are interested in research in the academic side
of music?
I would advise students to take my classes and those of my colleagues in musicology. This will
provide an opportunity for students to get to know us and the field in general, while helping them
explore their own interests.
 Also, I would recommend learning one or more foreign languages. To actually advance
knowledge and conduct your own original research rather than reinterpreting already known
documents, you have to be able to interact comfortably with manuscript materials that cannot be
rendered into English by Google Translate and also to converse comfortably with colleagues
and librarians who do not know English.
