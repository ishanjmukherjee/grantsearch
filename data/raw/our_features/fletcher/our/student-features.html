Kyra Lesmerises

SoC '26 - SURG

Kyra Lesmerises

Please provide a brief summary of your research: For my research, I studied female stand-up comedians and their style of comedy, and also the material they talked about in their sets. I attended many stand-up shows in the Chicago area, and took notes on the jokes of these comedians. I also interviewed four female comedians, and asked them their thoughts on their comedy career, and how they believe women are perceived in comedy. My research was focused on if women used anecdotal storytelling more frequently, and if their material was more frequently related to dating/sex/relationships. I concluded through analysis of the jokes I heard that storytelling was used more frequently among female stand-ups, while women did not talk about dating/sex/relationships more than they did about other topics. My interviews corroborated this info, with the comedians I interviewed stating that women are perceived as talking about those topics more, even when male comedians talk about those topics all the time, highlighting the biases and stereotypes that come with being a woman in comedy.

What made you initially interested in researching your project in particular? I took a stand-up class during winter quarter of my junior year, and I really enjoyed it. Near the end of the class, I thought about my stand-up and the style I leaned toward and the topics I talked about. I then thought about the differences between the performances of the female students in my class versus the male students, and began to wonder about the trends in stand-up done by women.

What made you interested in pursuing (interdisciplinary) research more broadly? I had never done research before, and it seemed like a really interesting opportunity to explore my passion in a lot more depth. I also thought that my major and my passions didn't really lend itself to research, but once I learned that I could do a creative research project, I decided to apply. It seemed like a way to stretch my brain in a way that I hadn't before.

Describe your experiences with research thus far. Was it tricky? What skills do you think you’ve gained? It was tricky to get started at the beginning. I knew what I had outlined in my proposal, but when it came time to actually start, I got nervous about reaching out to comedians and interviewing them. However, once I made my first contact and had a great experience interviewing them, I became a lot more confident, and even excited to conduct these interviews. I really honed my interviewing skills, specifically when it came to crafting my questions to get specific and really interesting answers.

Any tips or advice you have for students similar to you that are interested in pursuing undergraduate research? If you are a in a major similar to mine, don't think you can't do research! If you have an interest, you can find a way to ask a research question and then study it. When you are really passionate about your topic, the research doesn't feel like work, but rather a really cool opportunity to learn more about this topic that you enjoy so much!

Charlie Du

McCormick '29 - URAP

Charlie Du

Please provide a brief summary of your research: My research focuses on forecasting dynamical systems, which are systems that change over time, such as vibrations in structures, weather patterns, financial markets, and the spread of diseases. Traditional models often struggle to take full advantage of the large amounts of data available today, and they rarely tell us how reliable their predictions are. To address this, our work develops a new data-driven approach using neural operators, a modern machine learning technique that learns how entire systems evolve over time. Beyond predicting future behavior, our approach also estimates how confident those predictions are by drawing on statistical ideas such as quantile regression and Bayesian methods. This allows the model to capture both errors in the data and limitations in the model itself, producing predictions along with confidence intervals.

What made you initially interested in researching your project in particular? AI is increasingly becoming an integral part of our daily lives, so I was interested in doing something related to machine learning because machine learning is what we use to train AI.

What made you interested in pursuing (interdisciplinary) research more broadly? What drew me to interdisciplinary research is that the problems I find most interesting rarely fit inside a single field. My work on uncertainty quantification for dynamical systems sits at the intersection of machine learning, statistics, and the physical sciences, and it only works because ideas from each inform the others. Coming from computer engineering, I enjoy moving between the theory and the implementation, and I have found that the most useful insights often come from borrowing a concept from one area and applying it somewhere it was not originally meant to go. 

Describe your experiences with research thus far. Was it tricky? What skills do you think you’ve gained? Yes, research takes a lot of time, but it is rewarding in the end. I have developed strong analytical skills, learning how to break down complex problems, question my assumptions, and work through challenges methodically when results do not turn out as expected. I have also become much better at communicating technical ideas, whether that means writing clearly for a paper, presenting my work to people outside my field, or explaining my reasoning to mentors and peers. Beyond the technical side, research has taught me patience and persistence, since progress and results often come slowly.

Any tips or advice you have for students similar to you that are interested in pursuing undergraduate research? Do not wait until you feel fully qualified to do research! Reach out to as many professors as you can. Read about their work first, and if their research aligns with your interests, send a short, genuine email explaining what drew you to their lab and ask to set up a meeting. Research is where you learn, and showing curiosity and a willingness to put in the work often matters more than having all the right experience up front.

WCAS '28 - AYURG + URAP

Rosa Saavedra

Please provide a brief summary of your research: My research through the Lab for Accelerating Clinical Technologies (ACT) began with FoodSteps, a behavioral health intervention app designed to support individuals experiencing food insecurity and binge eating through one-on-one coaching and additional resources aimed at reducing binge episodes. I also explored alternative descriptors that may be more reflective of lived experience and preferred by participants. The broader goal of this research is to improve self-help resources, increase access to care, and enhance engagement with interventions such as FoodSteps among underserved populations.

What made you initially interested in researching your project in particular? Growing up, I visited family in Venezuela nearly every summer until conditions in the country became too dangerous. It placed me in a unique in-between space: while my own family was relatively stable, I was regularly exposed to the realities of food insecurity and limited access to resources affecting many people around us. Seeing how these challenges impacted individuals I cared about, from an early age, I was curious about how health issues disproportionately affect underserved populations and the broader effects of food insecurity on physical and mental health. At the same time, witnessing a close friend struggle with an eating disorder in high school gave me firsthand insight into how deeply these conditions can affect not only the individual, but also their relationships and overall well-being. Together, these experiences shaped my interest in understanding the intersection of food insecurity, eating behaviors, and health outcomes. When applying to URAP, I saw in the ACT lab an opportunity to explore these intersecting interests. They also drew me to the potential of telemedicine and technology-based interventions to improve access to care for individuals who may otherwise face barriers to treatment.

What made you interested in pursuing (interdisciplinary) research more broadly? As a Neuroscience major, much of my prior research experience from high school and my time at Northwestern before applying to URAP was primarily wet lab-based. Through these prior experiences I had grown familiar with working with various wet lab instruments as well as analyzing quantitative data. What initially struck me about the opportunity in the ACT lab at the time, aside from its mission, was its use of qualitative and mixed methods data (fusion of both qualitative and quantitative). The concept of pulling out conclusions from words felt so foreign to me and going into it, I was skeptical and curious about how it would be done.

In my time in the lab I have learned that qualitative data provides context and answers that quantitative data alone can not fully capture. While numbers and graphs are powerful tools for identifying patterns and outcomes, examining participants’ stories, struggles, and experiences with health interventions has offered a deeper understanding of how individuals engage with care and develop confidence in their ability to change their outcomes. In my independent project, I saw firsthand how integrating different methods can lead to more nuanced, patient-centered research. This experience broadened my perspective on research and bridged the gap between science and people.

Describe your experiences with research thus far. Was it tricky? What skills do you think you’ve gained? I have been incredibly blessed in my research experiences thus far. Throughout my time in research, I have benefited from a strong support system of undergraduate research advisors (shoutout Megan!), knowledgeable and patient principal investigators, and collaborative lab communities that have encouraged my growth (and encouraged that I make mistakes!). At the same time, research has not been void of challenges. Balancing coursework with lab responsibilities requires intentional time management, realistic expectations, and clear communication about the time and energy I can commit on a weekly basis. Over time, I have become much better at managing competing priorities, adapting to unexpected setbacks, and asking for help when needed. However, engaging in research has also opened doors to opportunities I may not have otherwise had. Through my involvement in research, I have presented posters, gained manuscript-writing experience, and even written grants, such as for AYURG. These experiences have given me skills that transcend the lab environment. I have learned how to communicate complex ideas to diverse audiences, collaborate with people who are also passionate about research, and become more comfortable stepping into unfamiliar or challenging situations. In many ways, research has pushed me to grow in ways I never anticipated.

Any tips or advice you have for students similar to you that are interested in pursuing undergraduate research? My biggest piece of advice would be to take full advantage of the resources your school provides. The research advisors were super helpful throughout my experience and can be great guides when navigating opportunities, applications, and uncertainty. I would also encourage students to be open to research outside of what they initially expect or even outside of their major. As a Neuroscience major, stepping into psychology and behavioral health research exposed me to entirely new ways of thinking, methodologies, and questions that I otherwise would not have encountered. Being willing to try something unfamiliar taught me a lot skill wise, and ultimately broadened my perspective as a researcher and a person.

Please provide a brief summary of your research.
I investigated the Gay Voice speech stereotype and created a web experiment testing if s-type sound features are part of that stereotype. Straight American actor Darren Criss was used as auditory and visual stimuli because of his famous portrayal of a gay character on Glee. Using visual primes in an experimental context can be tricky because facial expressions, hair styles, facial features, and other factors influence what social meaning we detect from the prime; since there existed a photo of Criss marrying his wife in real life and his character marrying a man on Glee, I used him to solve this problem. He was the source for straight and gay visual stimuli, and I used a neutral profile avatar as a control. Ultimately, there wasn't any support that the s-type sound feature I tested is not part of the Gay Voice stereotype, and the theoretical explanation is because specific phonetic features cn mean multiple different things and are used creatively to communicate (and perceive) complex identities in speech.

What made you initially interested in researching your project in particular?
I've always liked learning about the mind sciences, but one of the most interesting things I learned in a linguistics class is that listeners; perception of a speaker's voice can actually change depending on their expectations of how they speak. When talking to someone of a specific race, gender, sexual orientation, or nationality, our expectations of how those groups talk can "override" how we sense their voices!

Please provide a brief summary of your research.
My research explores how disrupting circadian rhythms impacts gut inflammation, using a mouse model of ulcerative colitis. I induced colitis in mice by giving them dextran sodium sulfate (DSS) in their drinking water, and compared disease severity between wild-type mice and mice with a Clock Δ19 mutation, a genetic alteration that disrupts their circadian clock. We tracked clinical symptoms daily, including weight loss, stool consistency, and rectal bleeding, and statistically analyzed the progression of disease. The goal was to investigate whether a misaligned circadian rhythm makes colitis worse; a question with real-world relevance for shift workers and patients with IBD.

What made you initially interested in researching your project in particular?

Please provide a brief summary of your research.
My research in the Geraci lab looks to implement a Horizontal Fiber Interferometer (HIFO) within the ARIADNE experiment. Within ARIADNE, an experiment searching for a well-motivated dark matter candidate the Axion, a rotating source mass is utilized to generate a pseudo magnetic field carried by the axion, affecting hyper-polarized helium-3 gas. Due to the design and nature of the experiment, the source mass will have a slight wobble to its motion, but this wobble must not exceed 75 microns. A method to detect this, the HIFO, is necessary to readout the displacement of the rotor in situ, under cryogenic vacuum conditions reaching 4K and 10E-6 atm. During my past research, it was found that there is a direct one to one relationship between measured visibility and distance from the rotating mass. My past work has also shown this direct relationship can be utilized as a "ruler," precisely measuring the displacement of the source mass on the order of several microns. Over the past year and a half, my research has pursued designing, implementing, testing, and utilizing this HIFO to accurately measure the displacement of the source mass for eventual data taking experimental runs of the larger ARIADNE experiment.

My focus was pre-clinical research concerning a promising potential therapeutic to alleviate Renal Ischemic Reperfusion Injury (IRI), an unavoidable injury in the organ transplant setting. I then investigated the effect of the potential therapeutic on these same factors. The therapeutic is an siRNA knockdown of XBP1, a vital transcription factor in the ER stress pathway that results in inflammation. Compared to non-transfected cells, TECs transfected with XBP1 siRNA displayed less inflammation, kidney injury, and cell death imaged/quantified via the same assays used previously. This emphasized that the knockdown of XBP1 in TECs is a promising potential therapeutic to alleviate IRI.

I found it particularly compelling that this type of injury (IRI) is quite unavoidable during/after transplantation and that we somehow have not developed any effective solutions to this injury. Please provide a brief summary of your research.
My research this summer focused on determining whether mixtures of peptide amphiphiles (PAs) with the peptide sequences A2G2 and V2A2 would result in molecularly mixed or phase-separated (non-mixing) supramolecular nanofiber assemblies. To test this, I utilized variable temperature polarized light microscopy (VT-PLM) to record the temperature at which PA fiber assemblies transitioned into spherical micelles. This transition temperature was denoted by the complete loss of birefringence signal, produced by anisotropic nanofiber assemblies, indicating a complete transition to isotropic spherical micelles.

Based on previous work, the A2G2 peptide sequence is known to form weak β-sheets that produce less cohesive nanofibers, leading to lower transition temperatures. In contrast, the V2A2 sequence is known to form strong β-sheets and more cohesive nanofibers, resulting in higher transition temperatures. Thus, increases in mol % composition of A2G2-based PAs mixed with V2A2-based PAs were expected to decrease cohesion within fiber nanostructures and therefore decrease the transition temperature. This trend was seen in my past work on mixtures of A2G2E2 with V2A2E2 PAs and A2G2E4-GIKVAV with V2A2E4-GIKVAV PAs, which indicated some degree of mixing between the PA pairs.

I chose to further analyze this trend by altering the equivalents of NaOH added to PA mixtures of the A2G2E4-GIKVAV with V2A2E4-GIKVAV PAs, which were originally prepared with 4 equivalents of NaOH. This mixture was selected in particular to better analyze the effect of epitopes like GIKVAV on the mixing of PAs. As greater equivalents of NaOH tend to reduce cohesion within a fiber assembly and therefore reduce the transition temperature, I was curious to see if the same transition temperature trend would persist in mixtures of 3 and 3.5 equivalents of NaOH as well. Under VT-PLM, the same trend in transition temperature was observed for all equivalents of NaOH, noted by a more rapid decrease in birefringence signal intensity with increasing % A2G2E4-GIKVAV. In addition, for the 30% and 50% A2G2E4-GIKVAV samples at all NaOH equivalents, there appeared to be multiple inflection points along the heating profiles; however, these inflections became less distinct with greater equivalents of NaOH. These inflection points suggested that there was a two-phase mixture at intermediate compositions of the two PAs, i.e., the appearance of a miscibility gap at lower equivalents of NaOH. Therefore, this further confirmed that rather than PA mixtures forming either fully phase-separated or completely molecularly mixed fiber assemblies, there seemed to be an upper limit on the degree of mixing between the different PAs.

Please provide the tile and a brief summary of your research/conference presentation.
Title: Modeling Individual Sleep Needs from Wearable-Device Data An individual’s natural sleep and wake cycle plays a critical role in nearly every aspect of their daily life. There are currently thought to be two processes that interact to regulate one’s sleep/wake cycle: a sleep homeostat, which depends on the amount of time you have been awake; and the circadian rhythm, an internal 24-hour molecular clock present in nearly every cell of the body. Both processes differ between individuals, whether that’s from a decision to pull an all-nighter one day or from a genetic inclination towards being an “early bird” or a “night owl”. This project focuses on building a model that can predict how particular sleep disruptions impact an individual’s sleep cycle, as well as their ability to recover from these disruptions. These processes have been studied and modeled using mathematical equations, which I was able to rewrite as more biologically realistic equations, and implement as code, resulting in a computational “two-process” model that can simulate a theoretical sleep-wake cycle with disruptions. Once we have a model that can use an individual’s data to predict their optimal sleep despite disruptions, it could be used in the field of sleep medicine to identify and address parameters affecting those with sleep disorders. Given its versatility, the model could also be used by anyone experiencing disruptions due to travel, social events, work, or family, and provide information about how to get sufficient sleep regardless.

Please provide a brief summary of your research.
Past research in syntax has shown that a phenomenon called "grammatical illusion" exists in English. "Grammatical illusion" happens when native speakers of a language erroneously accept ungrammatical sentences. For example, in "The key to the boxes are on the table" (Bock & Miller, 1991), the plural noun, boxes, makes readers erroneously accept this sentence. More specifially, researchers have found grammatical illusions in English "locative constructions," which refer to sentences like "Mary put chocolates in a container" or "I poured water into the cup" (Kim & Yoshida, 2016). So far, there has been no related research in Mandarin Chinese. In my research, I aim to answer the following questions though studying Chinese locative constructions: Do grammatical illusions exist in Mandarin, or is the phenomenon only observed in English? If it does, in which environments are such illusions most prevalent?

Please provide a brief summary of your research.

Broadly, my research focuses on testing the antimicrobial properties of different types of materials, both porous and non-porous, and evaluating their potential for usage in real-world environments, such as healthcare facilities and public transportation. Heavy metals such as copper and silver are known to have inherent antimicrobial properties, which has led to recent advancements in copper-coated and metal nanoparticle-containing materials. How environmental factors such as humidity could enhance or diminish the antimicrobial properties of these novel materials is poorly understood. My work aims to establish the antimicrobial efficacy of copper-coated stainless steel and metal nanoparticle-containing textiles under different humidity conditions, as well as determine how bacteria might respond to exposure to these materials. The testing process is actually quite simple: I apply bacteria to the materials in question and allow them to sit on the materials in a specific humidity environment for a given length of time. I then recover the bacteria from the materials and quantify the amount of surviving bacteria relative to the initial amount of bacteria applied. So far, I've found that the antimicrobial efficacies of both copper-coated stainless steel and metal-containing textiles are not significantly affected by changes in humidity. In the future, I will investigate using artificial saliva as the bacteria-containing medium, which contain salts and small molecules known to affect the viability of viruses. Ultimately, these test results will help improve our understanding of when and how to use heavy metal-containing materials in healthcare, commercial, and residential facilities for the benefit of public health, and reveal considerations we should make before replacing traditional materials with antimicrobial ones to prevent transmission of COVID-19 and other pathogens.
