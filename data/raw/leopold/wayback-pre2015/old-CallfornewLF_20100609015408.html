LEOPOLD FELLOWS—Call for Applications for 2010-2011

 

The Nicholas D. Chabraja Center for Historical Studies at NU announces the third year of its undergraduate program honoring the teaching and scholarship of Professor Richard Leopold, a long-time member of Northwestern University's Department of History.

 

The program provides a small group of able undergraduate students with an opportunity to engage in genuine historical research. Leopold Fellows will work on current faculty research projects, learning how to interpret archival and documentary materials. Successful candidates should demonstrate an interest in learning how to interpret complex primary data. Working under the guidance of a member of the Department of History, the Leopold Fellow will learn how scholars develop arguments out of diverse research materials.  

 

 Each Leopold Fellow will receive financial support as a Research Assistant (at $9 per hour for a possible average of 8 hours a week). The program should not be confused with Work-Study. Financial need will not be considered in the selection process. The program may also fund travel or other expenses incurred by the Leopold Fellows.

 

Students may apply to be Leopold Fellows for two or three quarters, including the summer quarter. The Leopold Fellows will meet formally as a group once a quarter to discuss their experiences. At the end of the fellowship term a completed survey form and a short report on the substance of the research are required.

 

Application process: Please look over our list of faculty projects below and if interested apply for a Fellowship.  History faculty may nominate students to apply or interested students may apply in response to a specific faculty project. In either case, please provide the following information:

 

  • one-page resume with name, year, and contact information
  • list of history classes taken (with grades received)
  • short statement about why you would like to pursue a Leopold Fellowship, indicating on which ONE or TWO projects you would like to work and what quarter(s) you are available.

Please send applications to Assistant Director Elzbieta Foeller-Pituch via e-mail at efp@northwestern.edu (doc or pdf files, NOT docx files) The deadline for completed applications is Wednesday, APRIL 7 by 12 noon. Announcement of successful candidates will occur in the first two weeks of  May.

 

Questions should be directed via e-mail to chs@northwestern.edu or phone (847-467-0885). Our office is currently located at 1800 Sherman Avenue, suite 404, Room 415. The CCHS website is at www.historicalstudies.northwestern.edu.

 

Please note: If the Leopold Fellow fails to work closely with his/her mentor or cannot carry out the proposed research assignment in a timely fashion, after consultation with the CCHS Director he or she may be dropped from the program.

1. Michael ALLEN—Reining in Presidential Power

I am seeking a student with an interest in and knowledge of recent US history to assist me in the early stages of a research project on Congressional and grassroots efforts to rein in presidential power, particularly in the foreign policy arena, from the early 1970s through the late 1980s.  The student would help me to locate and make my way through relevant source materials, beginning with memoirs, reportage, and legislative records, and would also help me to develop a timeline and database of important moments, players, and archives along the way.  We will begin by identifying key players and events using published materials, gradually working our way out to investigate unpublished and archival sources.  Though most of the student's work will involve sources available in area libraries or through interlibrary loan, archival research may be possible depending on our progress.  Expertise with computer spreadsheet and note-taking software is helpful, along with a sense of creativity, initiative, and attention to detail.

2. Henry C. BINFORD—Small Business in Poor Urban Communities

The work to be done arises from my current research on nineteenth-century slum communities and bridges into work that I plan to undertake in the future.  The student researcher will use statistical, library, and archival resources to study the lives of immigrant and African American small business persons in poor areas of Chicago and Cincinnati in the mid-nineteenth century.  The student will begin by doing some basic reading about these communities, in both secondary works and in the manuscript I am writing.  Then, using materials I have already gathered, together with information from the Northwestern and other area libraries, the student will undertake a study of one or perhaps two wards in the cities mentioned above.  He or she will create collective biographical databases (prosopographies) containing basic census information from 1860 about all of the immigrant and African American individuals who own real estate and are engaged in low-level entrepreneurial work (storekeepers, saloonkeepers, coal dealers, boarding house keepers, laundry owners, etc.).  The student will then look for these individuals in other kinds of records tax lists, property deeds, directories, political and legal records -- expanding the collective biography to allow an examination of careers.  The researcher may need to spend a few days in Cincinnati to access records only available at the Cincinnati Historical Society and the Hamilton County Courthouse.  The overall goal will be to trace the lives of these individuals backward and forward in time, asking how they acquired property and how their situations changed, and comparing people of different backgrounds, men and women, residents of different places.  I will help the student to use this information to address larger questions: What role did small stakeholders from minority backgrounds play in the larger political and social life of their cities? To what extent did property give them leverage, either formal or informal?  How did they make use of the ward-based political systems then dominant in these cities?  How did they engage with the powerful business and political leaders of the civic elites?  To what extent did these obscure entrepreneurs shape the physical and social development of the city?  The student will conclude the work by writing a report on his/her findings.  I will work closely with the student at all stages of this process.

 

*** Fall 2010 and Winter 2011. No foreign language requirements.

 

3. T.H. BREEN—George Washington and the Creation of American Nationalism

Almost as soon as he became President of the United States, George Washington set off on a long trip of extraordinary significance for the nation.  During the fall of 1789, he traveled from New York, then the capital of the new republic, to the modern state of Maine.  And then, during the spring of 1790 he took a more ambitious journey from New York to Georgia.  In the tradition of Early Modern European monarchs who organized "progresses" throughout their kingdoms, Washington planned a "republican progress."  Aware from the battle over the ratification of the Constitution that the American people were deeply divided, he understood almost intuitively that he represented the hopes for the country, and by visiting thousands of ordinary men and women he aimed to set his own unifying figure against the divisive local interests that threatened the republican

experiment.  In this sense, he possessed in the fullest sense what Max Weber meant by charisma.  A Leopold Fellow assisting me would trace Washington's journey, noting in scattered town histories and regional newspapers how his appearance affected the political culture. The student should possess basic research skills and familiarity with the interpretation of primary sources.

 

*** Whole year, no foreign language requirements.

 

4. Gerry CADAVA—Building Modern Borders: A Physical, Spatial, and Cultural History of the U.S.-Mexico Border Since World War II

I’m beginning work on a new project about the history of the U.S.-Mexico border since World War II, and I would like a Leopold Fellow to help me during its early stages of development. Despite the argument that the border became more rigid and militarized after the war, the border has never become a Berlin Wall-like barrier. Rather, it has remained a hodgepodge of rivers, barbed wire, steel walls, observation towers, customs houses, international gateways, and works of art. My project will be about the construction of these various materials that make up the border. In one sense, by writing this history of the construction of the modern border, I aim to critique work on the border and borderlands that has approached these subjects rather imprecisely. But Im also interested in writing a comparative history of several border cities, something that historians have not done before.

I would like help collecting newspaper articles from U.S. and Mexican border cities, as well as in identifying state and national archival materials in both the United States and Mexico, which contain information about the construction of the border. This work will involve searching online databases, and may require the Leopold Fellow  to correspond with archivists and librarians.

*** Whole year or two quarters. Spanish speaker preferred.

6. Regina GRAFE—A Stakeholder Empire: the political economy of Spanish rule in 18th- Century America and beyond

The project works on a revision of the currently dominant narrative of Spanish colonial rule in the Americas and the Philippines. It argues that Spanish governance became increasingly a “stakeholder” model of political rule that co-opted sufficient local interests to minimize resistance. Implicitly, this is contrasted by an Anglophone imperial structure that could be characterized as a “shareholder” empire. It is a co-authored project undertaken with M.A Irigoin, and builds on a number of already published articles for a six-chapter book. The empirical base consists of an analysis of the fiscal accounts of the approximately 72 regional treasuries in the Spanish Americas as well as a large number of loans and other financial transactions between individuals, corporate bodies, Viceroyalties and the Crown. While we have collected most of the data, I would like to work with a student on two particular areas: a systematic analysis of public borrowing in Spanish, and a geographic analysis of some of the financial transfers.

 

The student would need some Spanish language (reading) skills, basic Excel, and a willingness to take a one day course in GIS software offered by the library. On the upside I think the project offers a the student some direct experience in geography oriented historical research, a basic introduction into what quantitative sources can do for a historian, as well as work with eighteenth century sources (photographed or transcribed). I would like to get started in the autumn.

 

*** Two quarters, preferably Fall 2010 and Winter 2011, but flexible. Spanish language skills needed.

9. Robert LERNER— An Edition and Study of a Fourteenth-Century Treatise

I am currently editing and commenting on a Latin treatise concerning the Great Schism of the West (1378-1416) that was written in 1385.  The basis for my project is that I have discovered a hitherto unknown manuscript copy of the treatise that dictates the necessity of a critical edition. (An edition based on a poorer manuscript copy was brought on in 1909.) Moreover, the treatise itself is an important neglected source: it was written by an author very close to the scene of the contested papal election of 1378. Although this author is avowedly partisan, he presents a very vivid narrative of events, full of circumstantial details, that has never been weighed sufficiently by modern scholars. Thus the purpose of the project is not only to offer a new edition but to vindicate the value of the treatise.

 

A student chosen to help work on this project must be comfortable in reading either Latin or French. Were the student to know Latin, his/her responsibilities would primarily concern the edition—comparing the manuscripts to decide on preferable readings. Were the student to know French his/her responsibilities would be to help determine places where the author’s account of events is original or diverges from other contemporary accounts. The number of quarters is flexible, depending on the candidate’s own time and qualifications.

 

*** Whole year or part. Latin or French required.

 

10. Kate MASUR—African American Federal Employees in Post-Emancipation America.

 A Leopold Fellow in the 2010-2011 year would assist me on a long-term research project titled In All Things American Citizens: African Americans and Federal Employment in Post-Emancipation America. This project traces the history and meaning of African Americans' federal employment from the Civil War through the early twentieth century.  The research focuses on three areas of employment: the postal service, the treasury department (including customs), and the army.  The project also involves the study of contemporary political parties, since federal appointments were often made through party patronage networks. Tasks for a Leopold Fellow would likely include reading nineteenth-century newspapers and manuscripts on microfilm and using published sources to identify African American federal employees and develop profiles of them.  A fellow might also accompany me on a trip to the U.S. National Archives to examine employment and Justice Department records.

 

Applicants should have a strong background in United States history and a keen interest in African American history. Attention to detail and willingness to use microfilm are required.  Expertise with spreadsheet and/or database software is desirable but not absolutely necessary.

11. Edward MUIR—The Italian Renaissance: The Virtues of Doubt/Local Knowledge

I would like a Leopold Fellow to assist me in researching my new book project. This is a book that aims to provide an overview of the Italian Renaissance that breaks with old conventions. The book opens unconventionally with an examination of religious expression in all its forms and ends with religious skepticism and, even more unconventionally for a study of the Italian Renaissance, the rise of commercial theater. That introduction and conclusion signal a distinctive approach to the period, which has usually been framed by political events and secular intellectual movements such as humanism. One of the lasting achievements of the Italian Renaissance was the creation of a powerful if elite culture that prized skepticism and anti-clericism within a regime of hegemonic Catholicism. It is also a period in which the liturgy of the Church neither declined nor disappeared, in contrast to much of northern Europe, but was reinvigorated and reasserted in highly theatrical forms that paralleled the rise of the new non-liturgical theater of commedia dell’arte and grand opera. The Italian Renaissance, it is argued, provoked neither the rise of secularism nor the decline of religion. Anti-clericism and even philosophical atheism became distorted mirror images of the triumphant Roman Church. These tensions between the Church Triumphant and its critics can serve as a metaphor for a broader set of contradictions that help explain the creative dynamism of Italian Renaissance society. An important running argument of the book is that social and ideological frictions stimulate doubt, a virtue that is the source of new ideas.  The fellow will be asked to investigate a series of historical questions and summarize texts. It will be real research.

*** Whole year. No foreign language is necessary but the ideal candidate might know Latin, Italian, or French.

 

12. Susan PEARSON—Registering Birth: Populations and Personhood in the United States of America

This research project (and, ultimately, book) traces the practice of recording birth from the early national period through the mid-twentieth century, examining the multiple and often overlapping reasons for (and methods of) tracking when, where, and to whom, babies were born. At the beginning of the nation, nonstate recording practices were both more immediate and more widespread than was official birth registration.  Using a diverse array of sources, the project will explore how and why families recorded births in bibles and diaries, plantation owners tracked the growth of their slave population, doctors kept private records – and sometimes registered – the births they attended, and churches recorded birth and baptism. Gradually, however, governmental registration supplemented and then supplanted local and informal practices.  This shift was neither accidental nor inevitable.  Rather, birth registration was the object of reformist attention and its ubuiquity the fruit of considerable labor. By the mid-1930s, the effort to achieve full registration had been largely successful.  States reformed their laws governing vital statistics collection, medical professionals and parents absorbed the notion that a birth was of interest to the government, and a variety of public and private institutions began to rely on the birth certificate as the foundational affixation of identity. A Fellow would help with two distinct research tasks.  First, the Fellow would travel to Washington, D.C. to use archival records from the Children’s Bureau located in the National Archives.  Second, the fellow would conduct magazine and newspaper research in Evanston to help identify significant events, legal reforms, and persons involved in the history of birth registration.

 

*** Summer & Fall 2010 & Winter 2011.  No foreign language needs.

 

13. Michael SHERRY—Go Directly to Jail: The Punitive Turn in American Life

The Leopold Fellow would assist me on a book project, "Go Directly to Jail: The Punitive Turn in American Life."  This project explores why the US has become a much more punitive nation over the past forty years, with the development of mass imprisonment--on a scale virtually unmatched elsewhere in the world--the most obvious expression of this punitive turn.  This is a project in the political and cultural history of the punitive turn, not primarily in its legal and judicial dimensions.   It examines the punitive turn both at the level of high politics (what presidents said and did) and at the local level (what communities did to acquire prisons, for example).  No special skills are required, but a good general sense of recent US history is vital. 

 

*** Whole year or part. No foreign language requirement.

 

14.  Carl SMITH—City Water, City Life

I am completing a major intellectual and cultural history of cities and water in the United States between the 1790s and the 1870s.  While this book, titled City Water, City Life, focuses on the establishment of the first successful water supply systems in Philadelphia, Boston, and Chicago, its central purpose is to explain how Americans living in cities thought and spoke about urbanization as it was happening, and, more specifically, how ideas mattered in fashioning the cities they created and the nature of modern urban life as we know it. Materials examined include a large and diverse body of sources, including visual as well as written materials.

 

I am seeking a Leopold Fellow to assist me with the following: reviewing and checking sources, including annotations and bibliography; location and review of additional relevant materials, including reading eighteenth- and nineteenth-century newspapers and periodicals; collecting biographical materials on the large number of individuals discussed in the book; reviewing and preparing the manuscript as a whole. This requires no special language skills, but general computer literacy, including Internet skills and access to a networked computer, are required.  Research experience is also desirable, but intelligence, proven success in history classes, judgment, good work habits, and dependability are the most important qualities I am seeking. I expect to travel to libraries in Boston and Philadelphia during the year, as well as to ones in Chicago.  If I am assigned a Leopold Fellow, s/he will definitely be asked to do research in Chicago; I do not know as yet if s/he will be needed to travel to other places.
