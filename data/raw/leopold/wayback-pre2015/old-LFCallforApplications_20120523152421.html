LEOPOLD FELLOWS—Call for Applications for 2012-2013

 

The Nicholas D. Chabraja Center for Historical Studies announces the sixth year of its undergraduate program honoring Professor Richard Leopold, a long-time member of the NU Department of History.

 

The program provides a small group of able undergraduate students with an opportunity to engage in genuine historical research. Leopold Fellows will work on current faculty research projects, learning how to interpret archival and documentary materials. Successful candidates should demonstrate an interest in learning how to interpret complex primary data. Working under the guidance of a member of the Department of History, the Leopold Fellow will learn how scholars develop arguments out of diverse research materials.  

 

 Each Leopold Fellow receives financial support as a Research Assistant (at $9 per hour for a possible average of 8-10 hours a week). The program should not be confused with Work-Study. The program may also fund travel or other expenses incurred by the Leopold Fellows.

 

Students may apply to be Leopold Fellows for two or three quarters, which can include the summer. The Leopold Fellows meet formally as a group once or more a quarter to discuss their experiences. At the end of the fellowship term a completed survey form and a short report on the substance of the research are required.

 

Application process: Please look over our list of faculty projects below and if interested apply for a Fellowship.  History faculty may nominate students to apply or interested students may apply in response to a specific faculty project. In either case, please provide the following information:

 

  • one-page resume with name, year, and contact information
  • list of history classes taken (with grades received)
  • short statement about why you would like to pursue a Leopold Fellowship, indicating on which

      ONE or TWO projects you would like to work and what quarters you are available.

1.Ken ALDER—A History of Things

For a project on the history of singular objects I am interested in hiring a Leopold Fellow to help me investigate the social history of strange and particular "things," among them Dali's Lobster telephone, the Unabomber's typewriter, Marie Curie's bicycle, Ben Franklin's bifocals, and a few others....  The aim of the project is to see how framing a history around a particular inanimate object can re-center our histories of people and social structures.  We tend to think of knowledge as moving from particularities to generalizations, but there have been scholars and scientists who have insisted on the opposite: that what is needed is a science of the unique.  We will investigate this possibility and its implications.  Students need not have any particular language skills, but they should have an active imagination and take pleasure in learning about individual things through research in the library.  

 

*** The fellow would be needed for the three quarters of 2012-13.  No particular language skill is required, just a willingness to plunge into detailed research.

 

 

2. Michael J. ALLEN—National Security Politics in the Long 1970s

I am seeking a student with an interest in and knowledge of recent US history and foreign affairs to assist me in my research on efforts to expose and reform abuses of presidential war powers from the late 1960s through the late 1980s. The student will help locate, process, and organize primary source materials. For instance, one project I envision would be to research the election campaigns of specific legislators who played a leading part in national security debates, starting with their first election to national office and their last election in which they suffered defeat. Similar in-depth research on key foreign affairs and national security correspondents and on leading journals is also envisioned, in addition to help with digital images brought back from archival research. Facility with Adobe Acrobat Pro and with NU library research tools is required, along with creativity, initiative, and attention to detail.

 

*** Whole year, with particular emphasis on the Fall quarter.

 

 

3. Henry BINFORD—Small Businesses in Poor Urban Communities

The work to be done arises from my current research on nineteenth-century “slum” communities and bridges into work that I plan to undertake in the future.  The student researcher will use statistical, library, and archival resources to study the lives of immigrant and African American small business persons in poor areas of Chicago and Cincinnati in the mid-nineteenth century.  The student will begin by doing some basic reading about these communities, in both secondary works and in the manuscript I am writing.  Then, using materials I have already gathered, together with information from the Northwestern and other area libraries, the student will undertake a study of one or perhaps two wards in the cities mentioned above.  He or she will create collective biographical databases (prosopographies) containing basic census information from 1840 through 1860 about all of the immigrant and African American individuals who own real estate and are engaged in some form of low-level entrepreneurial work (storekeepers, saloonkeepers, coal dealers, boarding house keepers, laundry owners, etc.).  The student will then look for these individuals in other kinds of records — tax lists, property deeds, directories, political and legal records -- expanding the collective biography to allow an examination of careers.  The researcher may need to spend a few days in Cincinnati to access records only available at the Cincinnati Historical Society and the Hamilton County Courthouse.  The overall goal will be to trace the lives of these individuals backward and forward in time, asking how they acquired property and how their situations changed, and comparing people of different backgrounds, men and women, residents of different places.  I will help the student to use this information to address larger questions: What role did small stakeholders from minority backgrounds play in the larger political and social life of their cities? To what extent did property give them leverage, either formal or informal?  How did they make use of the ward-based political systems then dominant in these cities?  How did they engage with the powerful business and political leaders of the civic elites?  To what extent did these obscure entrepreneurs shape the physical and social development of the city?  The student will conclude the work by writing a report on his/her findings.  I will work closely with the student at all stages of this process.

 

*** Whole year. Language: English.

 

 

4.  Deborah COHEN—“Gentlemanly Capital in the Argentine”

One-quarter of British overseas investment in the late 19th century (nearly a billion pounds) was in Latin America.  In 1914, there were more than 40,000 British passport-holders in Argentina, many more than in the Rhodesias.  This research project – a first foray into a new book on informal empire – considers the networks of so-called “gentlemanly capitalism.”  Drawing on new databases of British periodicals, the Leopold Fellow will investigate debates about Argentinian investments, track British commercial expansion, and compile a database of Anglo-Argentinian families.  [cont. on next page]

 

*** I would like to employ a Leopold Fellow for the entire year (2012-3).  I will be on leave in the winter and spring quarters, but except for a few trips to Britain, will be in the Chicago area, and will meet with the Fellow on a regular basis. I am eager to hire a student with substantial background in economics.  Skill in Spanish would also be a plus.

 

5. ROBERT LERNER—Biography of an Eminent Historian

I am in the midst of writing a biography of Ernst Kantorowicz (1895-1963), an important German-American intellectual. Kantorowicz was one of the greatest medieval historians of the twentieth century: his biography of the Emperor Frederick II (1927) was one of the most widely-discussed books of Weimar Germany, and his The King’s Two Bodies (1957) has influenced work in numerous disciplines and remains one of the best-selling works in history more than half a century after its publication.  Kantorowicz by no means only just sat at his desk. Quite to the contrary, he had an extremely eventful, often turbulent career. He fought in the First World War (battle of Verdun; Turkish front), and then in 1919, as a far-rightist, against the “reds” in Berlin and Munich. Until 1933 he belonged to the elitist circle of the charismatic poet, Stefan George.  Although a rightist, he was an ardent anti-Nazi and spoke against them courageously from the lecture platform in 1933. Then he was forced out of his professoriat, and emigrated to the U.S. In 1939 he gained a position at the University of California, Berkeley. By then he had moved to the left and in 1949/1950 he was one of the leaders of opposition to a loyalty oath imposed on the Berkeley faculty. Because he refused to sign the oath he was fired. Then he spent the final part of his career at the Institute for Advanced Study in Princeton. His Anglophone friends and correspondents included Maurice Bowra, Isaiah Berlin, Felix Frankfurter, J. Robert Oppenheimer, and George Kennan.

 

*** I would like to have a topflight student as a junior collaborator during what I hope will be the last year on which I will be working on this project. The main responsibilities would be filling in background information, ascertaining sources of citations, and checking to see whether there is any correspondence I may have missed. Reading knowledge of German is a strong preference but not a requirement. I am flexible as to the number of quarters the student might work, as well as which particular quarters they might be.

 

6. Kate MASUR—Runaway Slaves and the Abolition of Slavery during the Civil War

A Leopold Fellow in the 2012-2013 year would assist me on research about the demise of slavery in the Washington, D.C., region at the outset of the Civil War.  The project considers how slaves escaped farms and plantations in Maryland and Virginia; the nature of law enforcement in the District of Columbia, both before and during the Civil War; and the conflict between military and civilian power in border cities at the war’s beginning. Tasks for a Leopold Fellow would likely include reading nineteenth-century newspapers and manuscripts on microfilm; using databases such as Ancestry.com to track obscure individuals; and reading and interpreting congressional debates.  Travel to an archive might also be useful.

 

*** The fellow should have a strong background in United States history and a keen interest in African American history.  Students who have taken History 314 are especially welcome to apply.  Attention to detail and willingness to use microfilm are required.  Expertise with spreadsheet and/or database software is desirable but not absolutely necessary. Availability for three quarters is preferred, but two would also work.

 

7. Michael SHERRY—Go Directly to Jail: The Punitive Turn in American Life 

This project explores why the US has become a much more punitive nation over the past forty years, with the development of mass imprisonment--on a scale virtually unmatched elsewhere in the world--the most obvious expression of this punitive turn.  This is a project in the political and cultural history of the punitive turn, not primarily in its legal and judicial dimensions.   It examines the punitive turn both at the level of high politics (what presidents said and did) and at the local level (what communities did to acquire prisons, for example). 

 

*** No special skills are required, but a good general sense of recent US history is vital.  I am flexible about which quarters and how a Leopold Fellow would assist me but would make the greatest use of a Fellow during fall and winter quarters.  Depending on my needs and the Fellow's talents, some research travel might be involved. Fall 20011 and Winter 2012 or whole year.

 

8. Carl SMITH—Popular Music and the American City

The music publishing industry was an important component of cultural life in urbanizing America, issuing an enormous quantity of songs on a host of subjects, many of them having to do with city life itself.  I am seeking a research assistant in a new project that investigates the popular song in late-nineteenth-century urban America, focusing on Chicago.  To be more specific, I am at the outset of an examination of what kinds of popular music were written about and published in the city in this period as part of a larger study of how urban life was imagined and expressed.  I am looking for a student who can work with me in conducting a disciplined search for sheet music in local, regional, and online collections and a detailed classification and analysis of this body of sources. 

 

*** I am seeking assistance in the winter and spring 2013 quarters.  Musical skills are a plus, but I am mainly looking for someone with some knowledge of and interest in American urban history who is a resourceful and organized researcher.  This project will very likely involve work in Chicago collections, such as the Newberry Library.
