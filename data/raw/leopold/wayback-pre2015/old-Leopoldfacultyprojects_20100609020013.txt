Josef BARTON:
Crumbs of Fresh Bread for Everyone: Mexican Rural Women and the Reconstruction of Community

In the half century between 1880 and 1930, Mexican rural peoples entered into migratory agricultural work. In the larger context of twentieth-century North American history, the formation of this migratory work force was a crucial element in the making of two modern economies. Some of this is well studied.  But several pressing issues remain.  First, historians know remarkably little about how the rural poor actually confronted the changes that engulfed their communities. Second, they have little studied how the rural poor reconfigured and reconstituted communities in the face of physical and cultural dismemberment.  Third, historians still lack a full understanding of the ways that migration facilitated the recovery of rural communities.  And, finally, far too little of the work that does exist on these issues is comparative. This project aims to speak to these issues through a historical study of women and men in northern Mexico and the Southwestern United States between 1880 and 1930.  More specifically, my work seeks a historical understanding of womens reconstitution of rural communities: of their provisioning activities in sustaining their households and kin; of their reconstructing their communities in the face of disruption; of their entry into migratory work; and of their capacity to act collectively to reconstitute networks and communities at home and abroad. 

In my research thus far, I have uncovered much documentation in northern Mexican and US Southwestern repositories, public and private, local, state, and national. I am pro;posing to employ a Leopold Fellow to work through the inventories and documents in two enormous collections on 20th-century American labor: the AFL-CIO archives at the State Historical Society at the University of Wisconsin-Madison, and the CIO Secretary-Treasurer's archives at the Walter Reuther Library at Wayne State University.  Both  collections have printed and on-line inventories; both have microfilm editions of about half of the documents. The Leopold Fellow's research will have both a general thrust -- the labor-movement context of women's movements in the US Southwest -- and a specific focus -- the local efforts at women's unionization that stretch from 1907 to the early 1940s. Since both the AFL-CIO and the CIO Secretary-Treasurer's collections are immense, the Fellow's work will require both creativity and perseverance.  In addition, the Fellow's work will require a week's visit to Madison and a week in Detroit, to read the large cache of relevant files not yet microfilmed. Along the way, the Fellow will acquire many research skills, much knowledge about the Mexican and US borderlands, and, perhaps, develop a topic for independent research. If the Center will consider summer work, as the announcement contemplates, I would very much like the student to begin in July or August.

Henry C. BINFORD:
Small Business in Poor Urban Communities

The work to be done arises from my current research on nineteenth-century “slum” communities and bridges into work that I plan to undertake in the future.  The student researcher will use statistical, library, and archival resources to study the lives of immigrant and African American small business persons in poor areas of Chicago and Cincinnati in the mid-nineteenth century.  The student will begin by doing some basic reading about these communities, in both secondary works and in the manuscript I am writing.  Then, using materials I have already gathered, together with information from the Northwestern and other area libraries, the student will undertake a study of one or perhaps two wards in the cities mentioned above.  He or she will create collective biographical databases (prosopographies) containing basic census information from 1860 about all of the immigrant and African American individuals who own real estate and are engaged in low-level entrepreneurial work (storekeepers, saloonkeepers, coal dealers, boarding house keepers, laundry owners, etc.).  The student will then look for these individuals in other kinds of records — tax lists, property deeds, directories, political and legal records -- expanding the collective biography to allow an examination of careers.  The researcher may need to spend a few days in Cincinnati to access records only available at the Cincinnati Historical Society and the Hamilton County Courthouse.  The overall goal will be to trace the lives of these individuals backward and forward in time, asking how they acquired property and how their situations changed, and comparing people of different backgrounds, men and women, residents of different places.  I will help the student to use this information to address larger questions: What role did small stakeholders from minority backgrounds play in the larger political and social life of their cities? To what extent did property give them leverage, either formal or informal?  How did they make use of the ward-based political systems then dominant in these cities?  How did they engage with the powerful business and political leaders of the civic elites?  To what extent did these obscure entrepreneurs shape the physical and social development of the city?  The student will conclude the work by writing a report on his/her findings.  I will work closely with the student at all stages of this process.

T.H. BREEN
During the run-up to the American Revolution the coastal towns of  Maine (then part of Massachusetts) generated particularly radical and violent responses to British rule.  Samuel Thompson of Brunswick, for example, organized a small army and wanted to start fighting long  
before other Americans were prepared to take such actions.  Vigilante groups in Gorham and Scarborough hunted down suspected Tories as early as the summer of 1774.  The Leopold Fellow would examine antiquarian histories of these communities, searching for information  
that would allow a reconstruction of this violent political culture. Most of the research could be carried out on-line or through inter- library loan, but it is possible that an able assistant might be  
asked to visit the Maine Historical Society in Portland.

Benjamin FROMMER:
A student fluent in German is needed for the Fall 2008 to search German and Austrian press and literature for references to and opinions about Czechoslovakia and Poland, especially in regards to the treatment of the Sudeten and other eastern Germans before and after the war.  This is for an article that I am writing on the place of Czechs and Czechoslovakia in the German narrative of suffering.  It is my contention that Czechs play an outsized role in German memories of the war because, unlike the Poles, the Czechs can be cast as perpetrators of unjustified crimes against the Germans.  In German eyes, the Czechs "had it good" during the war and therefore Czechoslovakia had no justification for the expulsion of the Sudeten Germans afterwards.  (German exiles by contrast prefer not to talk about what happened in Poland during the war, thereby implicitly recognizing some justification for the expulsion from the "recovered territories.")  The result is a particularly potent narrative of Sudeten German martyrdom that posits Germans as victims and not merely perpetrators of the Second World War.  It's a narrative that still complicates international relations today.

Robert LERNER:
The project in question is an edition of letters written by and to the noted historian Ernst Kantorowicz (1895-1963). The edition in turn would help serve as a basis for a biography of Kantorowicz that I am ultimately planning. My projected edition would draw on letters in public collections as well as on a considerable number that are in my possession. Kantorowicz, a German emigre who taught at Berkeley (1939-1950) and then was located at the Institute for Advanced Study in Princeton until his death in 1963, is widely recognized as one of the most important and influential historians of the twentieth century, above all for his seminal book, The King's Two Bodies. In addition he was an important figure in American academic life as a result of his being one of the leaders of the opposition to a McCarthyite loyalty oath at Berkeley, which resulted in his dismissal. His letters  contain references to his role in the loyalty oath crisis and the writing of his major work; they also refer to current politics and very often are devastatingly witty.
        Research Assistant Responsibilities: The letters contain many references to the work of other scholars, and sometimes to current events that are not immediately easy to identify. All such 
references will need annotation, and I need a good research assistant to help with this job. The work would consist of looking up data in biographical repositories, historical surveys, and sometimes locating articles in journals to which Kantororowicz is referring. Some knowledge of German would be a plus because Kantorowicz frequently alluded to work in that language. But this is not an absolute requirement. It is too early to tell whether any travel would be necessary, but checking regional university archives (especially those of the University of Chicago) for hitherto unnoticed letters by Kantorowicz would not be out of the question. 

Edward MUIR:
The Unknowns: Skepticism in Early Modern Venice
I will be pursuing a research project on a Newberry Library Fellowship during the academic year 2008-2009. The project involves analyzing a group of thinkers who in early seventeenth century in Venice took advantage of the absence of press censorship to popularize a strain of religious and philosophical skepticism, which, I want to argue, directly influenced the Enlightenment thinkers of the eighteenth century. The central figures in promoting skeptical thought in Venice were the members of the Academy of the Unknowns, a debating society whose members published hundreds of books and pamphlets over a thirty year period, many of which were translated from Italian into French and English. The Unknowns, pioneers in what we would now call journalism, created an engaging literary style in their novels and histories that were intended to arouse passionate emotions rather than to convince through reason, a trait that helps account for their widespread influence at the time but also for the fact that their appeal is easily lost on modern readers. This project intends to recapture the world of the Unknowns, to analyze their writings, and to evaluate their appeal during the early modern period. I am looking for a student who has a very good command of Italian to help with reading and taking notes on some of the more than 150 books by members of the Unknowns available in Chicago. A considerable number of them are in the Northwestern University Library Rare Books collection, which is where the student will work. Since I will be working at the Newberry, I will be able to supervise the student on a regular basis.

Sarah PEARSALL:

The Battle for Monogamy: A History of Polygamy in America
This project is about polygamy and marriage in moments of cultural contact in early America.  I am seeking a Leopold Fellow who would be able to help me track polygamy and marriage references in travel narratives from early America and related areas.  I am interested in the portrayal of marriage practices among Native Americans, Africans, and Ottomans in these accounts.  The study of polygamy in early America provides a novel perspective and a deeper understanding of issues with which Americans (and others) continue to wrestle: who has the right to regulate and define marriage, and why.  Debates about polygamy often signal a clash of cultures, and this project investigates those moments of confrontation, considering how the organization of marriage and gender roles have been deeply implicated in defining barbarity and civilization in colonial projects. The Fellow would focus on the discussions of polygamy and marriage and gender roles more generally in accounts written by travelers in the early modern period.  Much of the material of interest is printed (including travel accounts from voyages to the Ottoman Empire and West Africa in the sixteenth to eighteenth centuries as well as travel accounts from North America, ca. 1600-1900).  There is an online database of American travel narratives that would be the first place to search.  There are also modern editions of travel accounts from this period. There are many printed travel accounts from this era now available online through the main library catalogue system.  Much of the work could thus be completed online.  However, it would be excellent if the Fellow might be willing to do more hands-on primary research in the Ayer Collection at the Newberry Library in downtown Chicago (travel and meal expenses would be reimbursed while working there).  There are manuscript travel accounts of visitors to various Native American nations, and I would like to know how marriage practices are represented in these accounts.  It would be an advantage, but hardly essential, if the Fellow could read French or Spanish, as there are relevant travel accounts from this period in these languages (though many are available in translation).

I envision several research tasks for a Fellow.  One, and probably first, would be some number-crunching about shifts from the 1970s onward between the uniformed military population and the population of those working or incarcerated in the criminal justice system; since I'm a dunce at number-crunching, the Fellow would need to have some skills on this front--I can provide some guidance about where to find the numbers, but not much about how to extract and manipulate the data.   A second task would be to find, save, and analyze key documents in public discourse, most now available in online form, concerning mass imprisonment and other aspects of the punitive turn.   A third would be archival research in presidential and other libraries--that is a less likely task, given the skills involved, but the first two tasks might well prepare a Fellow for the third; obviously, this task would involve travel expenses.  Given that this an ongoing project, my needs will evolve and may shift, but anything else I might require would at least be proximate to the tasks that I've outlined here.  The ideal student would have some grounding in recent US history and/or in the field of criminal-justice policy and history.
