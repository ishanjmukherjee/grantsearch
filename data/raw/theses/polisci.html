Evelyn Ahdieh | From Roe to Reagan: The Framing of Abortion in Congressional Hearings and the Development of Issue Positions

Abstract:  This thesis examines how moral, feminist, and medical frames have shaped the issue positions of the Democratic and Republican parties between 1973, following the Roe v. Wade decision, and 1989, through the Reagan administration. Drawing on congressional hearing transcripts and presidential documents, this inquiry uses Carmines and Stimson’s issue evolution model, along with Chong and Druckman’s framing theory, to argue that language at the elite level actively constructed the partisan divide over abortion. An analysis of this evidence illustrates the early dominance of the moral frame, followed by medical and feminist framing in a congressional setting that prioritized legitimacy. However, presidential remarks, used for the purpose of mobilization, translated elite debates to the public without the use of complex medical terminology. Taken together, these findings reveal how the abortion framing seen today originated at the congressional level, resulting in consequences for women’s health and reproductive rights.

 Thesis Advisor: Traci Burch

Philip Blumberg | “Americans aren’t serfs.” Institutional Investors, Nationalized Politics, and Congressional Housing Rhetoric

Abstract:  Institutional investors have purchased millions of single-family homes across the United States, concentrating ownership in Sunbelt and major metropolitan markets and generating significant public and political concern. Yet no research focuses on whether representatives respond to this structural transformation of local housing markets through political rhetoric. This thesis answers the question: do U.S. House representatives from districts with high institutional investor penetration adopt more housing rhetoric in congressional floor speeches? Using a dataset of 851 representative-Congress observations spanning the 117th and 118th Congresses and nine model specifications, I find that investor penetration does not predict corporate landlord, supply and zoning, or affordability rhetoric. Instead, median gross rent is a primary predictor of housing rhetoric, and Republicans discuss housing affordability significantly less than Democrats, suggesting representatives respond to visible cost conditions and partisan identity rather than local ownership structure. Four case studies illuminate the mechanisms behind the null on investor penetration, showing that policy entrepreneurship, committee position, and partisan ideology can drive housing rhetoric independently of local conditions. Even districts with among the highest penetration in the sample produce complete rhetorical silence when those channels are absent.

 Thesis Advisor: Chloe N. Thurston

Maria Chebli | The Politics of Uncertainty: Information and the Making of Learned Political Helplessness in Lebanon

Abstract:  Why does Lebanon’s political system remain resilient despite recurring crises, economic collapse, and widespread public disillusionment? Existing explanations emphasize sectarianism and clientelism, which account for elite endurance but do not explain how citizens come to believe that political change is impossible. I argue that Lebanese citizens’ perception of the information environment as fragmented, politicized, and crisis-normalizing plays a central role in producing what I call learned political helplessness—a cognitive expectation that political action will not alter outcomes. State opacity and partisan media generate confusion, distrust, and contradictory narratives, leading citizens to repeatedly witness failed reform efforts and normalize dysfunction. These dynamics erode citizens’ sense of both internal and external political efficacy and help explain why widespread frustration rarely translates into sustained collective action demanding change, thereby enabling the political system’s continuation. The October 2019 uprising serves as a powerful exception to the rule of learned political helplessness that helps us understand the mechanisms that typically underlie the absence of collective action challenging the political status quo. It was a brief moment when decentralized, cross-sectarian information networks appear to have temporarily altered citizens’ perceptions of the otherwise fragmented and politicized information environment by enabling the rapid circulation of shared narratives about corruption, accountability, and collective grievance outside elite-controlled media channels, and thereby, reactivated citizens’ belief that coordinated political action could meaningfully challenge the political status quo. Its rapid rise and collapse provide analytical leverage for tracing how shifts in information shape expectations of political possibility. Specifically, it helps explain how temporary informational coherence in the perceived information environment can reduce perceived barriers to action, and how its fragmentation rapidly reinstates expectations of political futility. To demonstrate how the perceived information environment produces and reinforces learned political helplessness, thereby stabilizing political continuity despite grievance, this study employs a multi-method within-case research design integrating qualitative content analysis of state and media outputs, process tracing of the 2019 uprising, semi-structured interviews, and an original nationally representative survey with an embedded experiment. This design allows the study to trace causal mechanisms linking perceptions of information exposure to political expectations while holding constant structural factors such as sectarian identity, repression, and economic hardship. The empirical findings provide partial support for the proposed mechanism linking perceptions of the information environment—particularly perceptions of crisis-normalizing narratives communicated through partisan media and political elites—to higher levels of cognitive learned political helplessness. Perceptions of the information environment—particularly crisis-normalizing narratives—are significantly associated with higher levels of cognitive learned political helplessness, although helplessness itself does not significantly predict willingness to engage in collective action. Taken together, the findings suggest that while the perceived information environment contributes to the internalization of political futility—by associating crisis-normalizing narratives with higher levels of cognitive learned political helplessness—this helplessness alone does not translate into greater or lower willingness to engage in collective action. The results therefore provide support for the informational mechanism shaping expectations of political possibility, while indicating that additional factors beyond cognitive helplessness likely shape whether grievances ultimately produce collective mobilization challenging the political status quo. Overall, the thesis advances a new lens for understanding political continuity in Lebanon: systems may persist not only through institutions and elite strategies, but through the internalization of powerlessness among the publics they govern.

 Thesis Advisor: Wendy Pearlman

Jessie Chen | East Asian Pathways To Democracy Through External Democratizing Pressure: A Qualitative Comparative Analysis

Abstract:  This thesis examines the interplay between external pressure and domestic conditions in the democratization of East and Southeast Asian states between 1980 and 2000. While the region experienced rapid economic growth during the late twentieth century, regime outcomes diverged significantly. As some states transitioned to democracy, others sustained durable authoritarian rule. Existing scholarship has often emphasized either structural modernization or external Western pressure but has paid insufficient attention to how these forces interact within the distinct historical and geopolitical context of East Asia. Building on the framework of linkage and leverage developed by Steven Levitsky and Lucan Way, this study investigates how domestic factors – including organizational power, economic development, and mass contention – condition external pressures (e.g., sanctions, aid conditionality, and integration into Western economic and cultural networks). Using crisp-set Qualitative Comparative Analysis (csQCA) across fourteen cases, the study identifies multiple equifinal pathways to both democratization and authoritarian resilience. The findings reveal that external pressure alone is insufficient to produce democratic transitions. Instead, democratization occurs through distinct causal configurations: modernization-driven pathways in states with high levels of economic development and Western linkage, domestically driven pathways where sustained mass contention compensates for weak external ties, and externally driven pathways where strong linkage and leverage interact with less favorable domestic conditions. Conversely, authoritarian resilience can persist in configurations characterized by high economic development, weak organizational power, and mass contention. By highlighting the conditional nature of democratization, this study demonstrates that regime outcomes in East Asia are shaped not by single causal factors but by the interaction between external pressures and domestic political conditions. These findings contribute to broader debates in comparative politics by refining theories of democratization and underscoring the limits of external influence in the absence of favorable domestic conditions.

 Thesis Advisor: James Mahoney

Hana Dedić | Faith, Finance, & Political Change: Islamic Banking and Context-Dependent Development Across the Global South

Abstract:  Islamic finance has grown into a multi-trillion-dollar global industry over the past few decades, shaping financial systems across both Muslim-majority and non-Muslim countries. Rooted in Shari’ah principles that prohibit interest (ribā) and emphasize risk-sharing, ethical investment, and social justice, Islamic banking is frequently presented as a transformative alternative to conventional financial systems. Amanah Advisors, a global Islamic finance advisory firm, argues that “Islam seeks to bring order and remove chaos, and Islamic Finance is there to bring order to chaos in the economy, markets and society” (Amanah Advisors ; n.d. [accessed May 1, 2026]). Proponents similarly argue that these features promote economic inclusivity, reduce inequality, and contribute to broader socio-political stability. Yet despite these claims, there remains limited systematic evidence evaluating whether Islamic banking consistently delivers on these promises across diverse political and institutional contexts. While some case studies highlight potential benefits, other examples point to shortcomings, including the replication of conventional debt-like structures, limited risk-sharing in practice, and uneven access to financial services across populations. This thesis therefore examines the extent to which Islamic banks achieve their stated objectives, particularly in shaping socio-political and developmental outcomes. The motivation for this project is informed not only by these broader debates, but also by a gap in academic exposure and empirical clarity. The persistence of practices that mirror conventional finance, alongside mixed developmental outcomes in countries with significant Islamic banking sectors– particularly the Gulf countries–highlights a fundamental tension between the theoretical ideals and real-world implementation of Islamic finance.

 Thesis Advisor: Zekeria Ahmed Salem Denna

Sofia Dymova | Why Did the 2014 and 2022 Sanctions Targeting Russian Oligarchs Fail to Achieve Their Intended Political Objectives?

Abstract:  Western sanctions against Russian oligarchs were built on a flawed reading of who actually holds usable political leverage in Putin’s Russia. Using an intrinsic explanatory case study of Russia, the thesis combines elite interviews with Western policymakers, analysts, and one senior representative of a Russian oligarch, as well as document analysis and secondary literature to reconstruct how oligarch sanctions were supposed to work and how plausible that mechanism was given Russia’s regime structures. It shows that Western officials imagined a relatively linear chain running from financial pressure on oligarchs to oligarchs’ pressure on Putin and resulting constraints on Russia’s ability to wage war in Ukraine. Yet the political realities of a sultanistic oligarchy – centralized decision-making, oligarch dependence on the Kremlin, coercive measures and extensive adaptation and evasion strategies – meant that this chain was never likely to hold. By tracing the mismatch between imagined and actual levers of influence, the thesis explains the limited impact of sanctions focused on oligarchs and offers more cautious expectations for using similar tools against authoritarian regimes.

 Thesis Advisor: Jeffrey Winters

Avery Shaw Franks | Stay In Your Lane?: How Media Narratives Approximate Factional Demand in Presidential Primaries

Abstract:  Presidential primaries are often described using the language of “lanes,” yet it is unclear what these categories represent. This thesis treats political lanes as media-constructed classificatory devices and asks whether they plausibly reflect underlying voter preferences. It analyzes a corpus of news coverage from the 2016 Republican and 2020 Democratic primaries alongside survey data from the American National Election Studies. Media texts are coded to identify recurring lane narratives, while voter preferences are mapped using clustering techniques. The findings show that ideological lanes align most closely with voter structure, while strategic lanes such as “establishment” or “electability” correspond weakly. Lane narratives are most prolific when elite coordination is weak and consolidate as coordination emerges. These patterns suggest that lanes may not always reflect voter demands, but they are narrative tools that vary in accuracy across contexts. The project clarifies how parties, media, and voters interpret competition under uncertainty.

 Thesis Advisor: Daniel J. Galvin

Camille T. Haines | When Americans Are Taken: Comparing U.S. Responses to State and Non-State Hostage Takers

Abstract:  This study aimed to examine how the type of perpetrator (state vs. non-state) taking an American hostage influences or alters the response from the U.S. government. Using a mixed methods approach including a small-N dataset coded from publicly available information, case study process tracing, and a survey analysis, this study found substantial support for the U.S. responding differently between state and non-state actors. The United States is more likely to engage in negotiations and offer concessions in cases involving state actors, while responses to non-state actors rely more heavily on coercive tools and exhibit greater variability. These differences are explained in part by varying credibility, legitimacy, and information asymmetry across perpetrator types. While public support for negotiation remains consistently high, attitudes toward concessions and coercive measures vary more, providing partial support for the relationship in public policy preferences and actor type. Despite the study’s limitations, the findings contribute to existing literature by demonstrating how the U.S. response differs, exploring how legitimacy and credibility shape U.S. decision-making processes, and offering a foundation for future research on hostage negotiation. Additionally, this study provides a step towards the fusion of terrorist studies with state-to-state scholarship in political science.

 Thesis Advisor: Daniel Krcmaric

Sarah Han | To Nuke, or Not To Nuke: How Leaders’ Dispositions Influence Major Nuclear Decisions

Abstract:  Why do some elite leaders acquire, test, or use nuclear weapons despite their costly effects, and why do other leaders not despite having the capability to do so? Current literature attributes leaders’ decisions to external factors, which explain the strategic motivations behind these leaders’ nuclear decisions. However, less is said about the role of disposition, or personality traits, and how they manifest into decisions that benefit the leader’s image but impose irreversible costs on the greater public. This thesis aims to study whether dispositional factors (narcissism and self-esteem) influence elite leaders’ nuclear decisions. I use process tracing analysis to conduct a three-way comparative case study consisting of Kim Jong-il (North Korea), Harry Truman (U.S.), and Eisaku Satō (Japan). Kim and Truman serve as positive cases (leaders who pursued major costly and/or escalatory decisions), while Satō will be my negative case (leaders who did not pursue major costly and/or escalatory decisions). I find that leaders who developed high narcissistic traits during childhood are more likely to pursue major escalatory nuclear decisions than leaders who developed low narcissistic traits during childhood. I also find that low self-esteem amplifies the effect of narcissism on a leader’s nuclear decision by increasing their need for validation. Finally, I find that when dispositions inconsistently appear across a leader’s life, situational factors may be a stronger explanation for their nuclear decision, especially when the situational pressure is high. Overall, my thesis argues that while disposition may be a contributing factor, it does not fully determine leaders’ decision to pursue major escalatory nuclear decisions.

 Thesis Advisor: Stephen C. Nelson

Katie Annemarie Keil | The Role of Elite Cues and Endorsements of Political Violence in Shaping Individual Attitudes

Abstract:  Recent acts of partisan-driven violence have raised questions about the extent to which individuals are willing to support these behaviors, especially when encouraged to do so by elected officials. Given the low support for extreme political violence (Holliday, Lelkes, and Westwood 2024; Kalmoe and Mason 2022), the current study focuses on less extreme forms and their attitudinal precursors, such as hostility linked to perceptions of dehumanization in partisan polarization. Previous research has also shown that meta-perceptions of support for political violence are a key mediator in shaping the relationship between exposure to violent rhetoric and individual-level attitudes toward political violence (Mernyk et al. 2022). Consequently, this study uses a survey experiment to examine how rhetoric from a Republican elite endorsing political violence affect broader perceptions of and individual-level support for political violence (SPV). An initial pilot study (N = 300) sampling only Republicans reported a null average treatment effect across all measured outcome variables, except for affective evaluations of the Republican Party—that of the speaker. Subsequently, the treatment produced no significant effect among Republican participants within the main study (N = 790), consistent with the conclusion that in-party elite cues have a null effect on the predicted outcomes. However, examinations of group-specific effects identified a significant treatment effect on meta-perceptions of the average Republican’s SPV and individual SPV among weaker-identifying Democrats. This suggests an alternative unanticipated hypothesis that runs contrary to our initial hypotheses, wherein partisan identity may moderate responsiveness to elite rhetoric through pathways otherwise neglected according to the standard framework.

 Thesis Advisor: Daniel C. Molden (Psychology) and Alexander Coppock

Kate Lautenberg | Ideological Expectations: How Candidate Conservatism Shapes Voter Preferences for Women in U.S. Executive Elections

Abstract:  While women US presidential nominees have only been seen in the Democratic party, punditry predicts the first woman president will be a Republican (Campbell and Lovenduski 2025; Simon 2024; Winter 2010). Similarly, in international politics links can be drawn between conservatism and electoral success for women executive candidates (Worthy et al. 2023). I dissect the interaction between conservatism and willingness to vote for women US executive candidates through two investigations. I conduct a historical study of this relationship using content analysis to provide an ideological score for US women governors since 1964. Regressing ideological score on female vote share provides preliminary evidence to suggest there is a positive correlation between conservatism and female vote share. Then, I conduct a vignette survey experiment to test whether voters are more likely to vote for a more conservative female presidential candidate compared to a less conservative counterpart. Greater conservatism is associated with a decreased likelihood of voting for a candidate among Democrats; the opposite is true of Republicans. The study highlights that more conservative women candidates are seen as more traditional and masculine. The results reveal that Democrats are less likely to vote for the more conservative party nominee compared to their less conservative counterpart, while Republicans are more likely to vote for the more conservative party nominee or are approximately indifferent between the two. However, when information is given about the level of traditionality and masculinity, these responses change. Democrats’ likelihood of voting for the more conservative candidate increases when they are portrayed as less traditional or less masculine; the reverse is true of Republicans. Taken together, these studies provide preliminary support to suggest that elements of perceived conservatism impact willingness to vote for women executive candidates; this effect differs between parties.

 Thesis Advisor: Mary C. McGrath

Sarah Lyons | How does rights-based climate litigation impact environmental policy outcomes?: Evidence from Colombia and Nepal

Abstract:  In the last decade, lawsuits invoking the constitutional right to a healthy environment have proliferated as a strategy to spur government action on climate change. Yet their effectiveness in shaping environmental policy remains unclear. This study therefore poses the question: how does successful climate litigation based on the right to a healthy environment ultimately affect environmental policy outcomes? I focus on two hypothesized causal mechanisms: the specificity of court orders and civil society pressures. To test these hypotheses, I conduct a qualitative case study of two successful rights-based climate lawsuits, one in Colombia and one in Nepal. I draw upon legal materials, government documents, and media coverage to process trace the links between litigation and policy change. I find that clear and actionable court orders, as well as sustained civil society advocacy, play an important role in driving environmental policy progress. However, courts are ultimately the most crucial actors ensuring post-ruling policy change. Overall, this study illuminates the conditions under which rights-based climate litigation is a productive strategy for motivating government action in the face of escalating climate threats.

 Thesis Advisor: Elizabeth L. Thom

Sarine Meguerditchian | Recognition without Responsiveness: Comparing the Turkish-Armenian and Armenian-American Communities

Abstract:  This thesis examines how classificatory systems shape political possibilities for ethnic minorities by comparing Armenian minorities in the United States and Turkey. Drawing on constructivist theories put forth by Rogers Brubaker, Ian Hacking, and Michel Foucault, this project analyzes how distinct regimes – American multiculturalism and Turkish nationalism – produce convergent outcomes of political marginalization for the Armenian ethnic community. This comparative study is based on 14 semi-structured interviews with American-Armenians conducted over Zoom with participants from across the U.S. and 14 interviews with Turkish-Armenians conducted in-person in Istanbul. Interviews were supplemented with ethnographic observations, secondary sources in Armenian studies, and archival research. In the U.S., Armenian-American legal classification as “white” enabled integration and institutional success for early Armenian immigrants, but renders current Armenian-American foreign policy claims as narrow ethnic interest demands. This limits policy success beyond symbolic recognition. In Turkey, Armenians are officially recognized as a religious minority under the Treaty of Lausanne, allowing for institutions to remain alive and functioning. However, suppression of ethnic identity and the criminalization of genocide discourse constrain open political advocacy. Across both contexts, recognition operates less as a form of political responsiveness than as a mechanism that limits political voice. These findings challenge the assumption that recognition leads to accountability, showing that classificatory regimes can shape whether states aptly respond to the minority communities they claim to represent.

 Thesis Advisor: Elizabeth Shakman Hurd

Max Outcalt | A Troubling Refrain: The Impact of the Northern Irish Troubles on Far-Right Ideology in the Republic of Ireland

Abstract:  As seen by the anti-immigration riots which ripped through Dublin in November 2023, far-right xenophobic sentiments have infiltrated the Republic of Ireland, a nation long-thought to be an outlier in this global trend. While existing scholarship often treats contemporary Irish xenophobia as a recent phenomenon, this study explores whether and how the political and social impacts of the Troubles may contribute to its emergence. This thesis involves the analysis of ten interviews with Dublin City Councillors conducted in 2024, utilizing a qualitative, interpretive methodology of thematic analysis and process tracing. Analysis shows that the Troubles, often relegated to simply a Northern Irish issue, did not directly contribute to the rise of far-right xenophobia in the Republic of Ireland. However, the moderation of Sinn Féin following the Good Friday Agreement did lead to the political isolation of its traditionally radical workingclass base. This disaffection, when combined with contemporary socioeconomic pressures, generated conditions in which far-right actors were able to mobilize support. By identifying this mechanism, the study hopes to provide a foundation for further research in both Irish politics and historical examinations of the effects of the Troubles.

 Thesis Advisor: William S.K. Reno

Jun Park | Residents’ Right to the City in Barcelona, Venice, and Seoul

Abstract:  This paper expands on the existing research and contributes more robust findings by investigating the gendered effects of austerity policy across countries and time. I answer the following question: “Do austerity policies disproportionately affect women?” In my exploration of this question, I consider social and political factors that may be influential. First I explain relevant findings in the literature. Next, I lay out my hypotheses which predict that austerity, or some elements of it, disproportionately limit women’s ability to pursue economic success. To evaluate my hypotheses, I employ a time-series cross-section regression to evaluate the relationship between fiscal adjustment and changes in gender inequality indicators across countries and over time. I find that there is a lack of a strong relationship between the economic gender gap and austerity measures. It is important to evaluate systems that support or exacerbate inequality, and this paper aims to do just that.

 Thesis Advisor: Nicole E. Wilson

Samantha Serban | Revisiting Mendelberg: Implicitness, Explicitness, and the Racial Politics of Messenger Identity

Abstract:  Scholars have long argued that the “norm of racial equality” constrains explicit racial rhetoric, leading political actors to rely on implicit appeals to avoid electoral sanction (Mendelberg 2001). This study reexamines that framework in a contemporary context marked by the increased visibility of explicit racial discourse. I hypothesize that explicit racial rhetoric will generate greater candidate sanctioning than implicit appeals, that White candidates will be sanctioned more than Black candidates for delivering racialized messages, and that racial resentment will reduce sanctioning among high-resentment individuals. To test these expectations, I conduct a preregistered survey experiment in which White respondents evaluate political candidates across variations in rhetoric type and candidate race in two policy domains. The results provide limited support for the expectation that explicit rhetoric produces greater sanctioning and only modest evidence that White candidates are penalized more than Black candidates. In contrast, racial resentment emerges as a strong and consistent predictor of candidate evaluation, with higher-resentment individuals significantly less likely to impose sanctions. These findings suggest that racial norms continue to shape political judgment but are unevenly enforced and increasingly dependent on individual attitudes.

 Thesis Advisor: Tabitha Bonilla

Chloe Shah | Framing the American Dream: Race, Merit, and Inequality in U.S. Billionaire Discourse

Abstract:  Economic inequality in the United States has reached historic levels, yet public faith in meritocracy and the American Dream remains remarkably durable. Felix Beilin | Repolonizacja Through an Empirical Lens: Content and Discourse Analysis of Private Media Capture in Poland

Abstract:  Between December 2020 and March 2021, PKN Orlen, the Polish national oil refinery,
completed a takeover of Polska Press, the owner of twenty regional daily newspapers with over
fifteen million online readers. To date, this has been the Law and Justice Party’s most significant
achievement within its campaign to “repolonize,” or ensure greater domestic ownership, of its
media. Allegations of media capture and democratic backsliding, as well as changes in article
content regarding coverage of controversial political stories, have proliferated throughout the
Polish news media ecosystem. They compel a longitudinal content analysis of pre-repolonization
and post-repolonization newspaper content to evaluate if and to what extent these highly political
consequences have been borne out. This study used content and discourse analysis to examine
changes in word count (as a proxy for article quality), frequency of name citation of politicians
and PiS affiliates, and the level of nativism across Polska Press newspapers and Gazeta Wyborcza,
a national-level control. Results indicated that article quality, as expressed through word count,
did not appear to fall at Polska Press newspapers, relative to Gazeta Wyborcza. Politicians at
Polska Press newspapers were named less frequently than before the intervention, and the rate of
mention of PiS figures fell dramatically as well – this outcome variable was somewhat muddied
by steep increases in politician and PiS figure name citation in Gazeta Wyborcza, which was meant
to serve as the control. The level of nativism in Polska Press articles, and especially in syndicated
articles across multiple newspapers, did not increase. Many of these findings are counterintuitive,
and invite more research into the nature of the Polish case of government media capture.

Nuo (Anor) Chen | Living through Belonging and Integration: Experiences Amongst Chinese Short-Term, Low-Skill Labor Migrants

Abstract: Existing literature on migration has largely failed to consider short-term, low-skill labor
migrants within the larger framework of integration and belonging processes, as policymakers and
political institutions assume that these processes only apply exclusively to migrants with long-term
settlement patterns (i.e., refugees). Consequently, policies and laws both in the US as well as abroad
have often excluded short-term, low-skill labor migrants from their integration/belonging policies
and resources. This research seeks to provide an exploratory study of the lived experience of short-term, low-skill labor migrants to evaluate if they do indeed undergo integration/belonging processes,
and if so, how short-term, low-skill labor migrants’ processes differ from long-term immigrants that
might be vital in guiding policy decisions and programs in the future. Through a qualitative, case
study analysis of Chinese short-term, low-skill labor migrants in the Chicago Metropolitan Area, this
study finds that short-term, low-skill labor migrants do indeed experience processes of integration
and belonging, with some overlapping similarities with long-term immigrants. However, despite some
initial similarities, their processes also widely differ from long-term immigrants and challenge
traditional models of integration posited by scholars like Ager and Strang. Despite its exploratory
nature, this study provides the groundwork for future research to build off of any initial patterns that
were identified and further provide the necessary visibility to the lived experiences of all short-term,
low-skill labor migrants.

Kelly Marie Miller | American Oligarchy? Billionaires, Polarization, and Tax Policy in the 2020 Senate Elections

Abstract: Over the past fifty years, U.S. tax policies have facilitated, rather than mitigated,
surging economic inequality, shifting massive amounts of wealth and income to those at the top
of the scale. The “median voter theorem,” first posited by economist Anthony Downs, suggests
that in an ideal democratic two-party system, moderate candidates from both parties tend to win
general elections by appealing to the center of the public opinion in their constituencies.
However, recent U.S. congressional elections seem to contradict this model. This thesis employs
a case study analysis of key 2020 Senate races in which six historically conservative
states—Kansas, North Carolina, Alabama, Texas, Georgia, and Tennessee—became noteworthy
battlegrounds. In all cases except Georgia and Texas, the candidates who won supported
economic policies, particularly on taxation and redistribution, that were becoming increasingly
unpopular among the public in their states but were aligned with the policy preferences of
ultra-wealthy conservatives. In order to offer a potential explanation for this outcome, I evaluate
the internal dynamics of the primary elections, such as low turnout, low visibility, and the
activity of ideologically-driven party activists and elites, which may have impacted the
nomination process. I also compare the policy preferences of major candidates and public
opinion on tax reform and redistribution in each state to the political contributions from 66 of the
top conservative billionaire donors during the 2019-2020 election cycle. Together, these elements
reveal that the candidates funded by these billionaires are the ones who are, more often than not,
elected and in many cases, more ideologically extreme than other primary and general election
candidates.

Andrew Drew Stephen Myers | The Rise of Zoom: Studying the Effectiveness of the Emerging Virtual Platform in U.S. Political Campaigns

Abstract: The Covid-19 pandemic introduced much of the United States to Zoom, a relatively
unused web-conferencing platform pre-COVID-19 pandemic, and forced millions of Americans
to rely on it for schooling, work, and daily communication between friends and loved ones. The
2020 presidential cycle was not immune to this phenomenon as all 2020 political campaigns
across the country shifted their get out the vote and fundraising efforts to Zoom and other web-conferencing platforms. The ongoing use of virtual platforms by political campaigns (and other
industries in the U.S.) even as the Covid-19 pandemic subsides, raises the question: how
effective are virtual political campaign events at turning out voters and fundraising when

participants are randomly assigned to attend a simulated campaign event in-person or over
Zoom. I find that differences between in-person and virtual mediums have no effect on voter

experience, all of which are in turn affected by the differences between in-person and virtual
campaigns. The study results showed that in-person campaign events performed better than
virtual ones in terms of both participant engagement and perceptions of the candidate. But given
the relatively small differences between the two, the clear benefits of virtual events, and both the
staying power and growth of virtual products, campaigns should remain open to the idea of using
virtual campaign events—when appropriate—in the foreseeable future.

The thesis finds that the contextual influence of one’s racial surroundings is real. In some circumstances, increased numbers of nearby blacks engender more positive views toward the out-group (i.e., blacks) and voting support for black political candidates, but not in all conditions. The effect differs depending on certain factors. Neighborhood population density and one’s pre-existing racial attitudes mediate the contextual effect of race.

Thesis Advisor: Reuel Rogers

 

Dylan Lewis | Unpaid Protectors: Volunteerism and the Diminishing Role of Federal Responsibility in the National Park Service

Abstract: This thesis explores the foundations of the extraordinary growth and importance of volunteers in the National Park Service. Using a coproduction framework, it explains why the Volunteers-in-Parks program has grown to its current state. Additionally, it explains why coproduction has prevailed as the response to the Park Service’s need for more resources. It is argued that a unique matrix of volunteer motivations creates a large supply of willing volunteers. Furthermore, the parks are shown to exist in a state of organizational poverty, which drives demand for unpaid workers. These supply and demand forces have fueled the growth of volunteerism. Finally, this thesis argues that theories of the hollow state and short-circuited democracy together explain why volunteerism has prevailed in the parks. It is concluded that hollow state volunteerism is inextricably tied to park health while short-circuited democracy volunteerism would subside if citizens became more aware of the important role volunteers play in the National Park Service.

Thesis Advisor: H. Paul Friesema
